Political identity drives trust in large language models

…even when accuracy is incentivized

AI bias: a supply-side literature, a demand-side gap

  • AI is now a primary interface for interpreting events, deciding, and forming opinions.
  • Supply side. A large literature documents bias inside AI — in model outputs and training (Friedman and Nissenbaum 1996; Garg et al. 2018; Vlasceanu and Amodio 2022; Bai et al. 2025).
  • Demand side. Almost no work on how users’ own political identities shape which AI systems they choose to use and trust.
  • With ideologically distinct models — Claude, GPT, Grok, Gab — now competing in public, which model a user picks is itself a political act.

Set the framing: the bias-in-AI literature is overwhelmingly about the supply side — what models produce. We pivot to the under-studied demand side: bias in user selection of AI. That distinction is the through-line of the talk.

Tools or media?

Question. When users choose between AI systems, do they pick the way they pick tools — for accuracy and performance — or the way they pick media — for ideological fit?

  • Tool view. Users prioritise accuracy. Differences in performance drive selection, especially under incentives. (Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey 2015; Glikson and Woolley 2020)
  • Media view. Users select congenial sources. Selection reflects identity even at a cost to accuracy. (Stroud 2011; Iyengar and Hahn 2009; Taber and Lodge 2006)
  • These views generate opposite predictions about what users do under accuracy incentives.

Two literatures, two predictions. Tool view: incentives sweep partisan signal away. Media view: identity persists. We test which one wins.

Why this matters

Stakes. Whether AI is a unifying or fragmenting force depends on how users select between systems, not just what the systems output.

  • Choice over information sources has historically driven audience segmentation, polarised trust, and fragmented ecosystems. (Sunstein 2001; Flaxman, Goel, and Rao 2016)
  • AI may be the next layer of that fragmentation — different groups relying on different systems to produce, interpret, and validate knowledge.
  • An optimistic counter-claim: AI could rebuild common ground (Tessler et al. 2024) — but only if users actually pick for accuracy.

The classic selective-exposure result is that media choice tracks identity. If AI selection follows the same pattern, the optimistic “AI as common ground” view doesn’t hold.

Design at a glance

2 × 2 between-subjects

  • Labelling: real names vs neutral colors
    • e.g. Claude / Grok / GPT / Gab vs. Purple / Blue / Green / Red
  • Order: political prompts first vs general-knowledge first

Sample: 1,884 US Prolific participants, demographically representative.

Mapping of color labels → underlying models randomised at the participant level and held constant within-participant.

Four models, by ideological tone

Model Position

Slants align with external user-evaluation work (Westwood, Sean J. and Grimmer, Justin and Hall, Andrew B. 2025).

Asymmetric design — there is no widely available “far left” model by design, so the extreme test exists only on the right. Mapping is randomised so that color labels cannot reveal underlying identity.

Task 1 — blind comparison of model outputs

  • Eight prompts: four political (e.g. “How common is voter fraud in the US?”) and four general knowledge (e.g. “What causes earthquakes?”).
  • For each prompt, participants press Generate, see four side-by-side responses, and pick their preferred one.
  • Outputs are prompt-engineered to differ systematically in ideological framing on political prompts; general-knowledge prompts produce substantively similar responses.
  • Outcome: per-prompt model choice → participant-level choice shares \(s_{im}\) and a most-preferred model.

Task 1 captures preference formation under repeated exposure. The split into political vs general prompts lets us see whether ideology-laden content drives sorting more than neutral content does.

Result 1 — preferences sort by party identification

Task 1. Republicans choose Grok +10.3 pp over Democrats, Claude −10.3 pp (both p < .001).

Task 2 (incentivised). Differences widen: Grok +19.9 pp, Claude −16.8 pp (both p < .001).

  • Independents fall between the two groups.
  • Omnibus party × model interactions strongly significant in both tasks (p < .001).

Preferences are not random — they are systematically structured by partisan identity, and the partisan gap actually grows when stakes are introduced in Task 2.

Result 2 — partisan divergence grows across contexts; naming sharpens it

Walk the audience through the figure: rows are party (Dem / Ind / Rep); columns split Unnamed vs Named; within each panel, model choice is shown across General prompts → Political prompts → Task 2. Trace the progression before pivoting to takeaways.

Result 2 — partisan divergence grows across contexts; naming sharpens it

Divergence widens General → Political → Task 2 — and persists under accuracy incentives. Brand labels sharpen it.

  • General prompts: party differences are modest — partisans broadly agree.
  • Political prompts: divergence widens sharply (Dems → Claude / GPT; Reps → Grok / Gab).
  • Task 2 (incentivised): divergence persists — accuracy stakes do not erase it.
  • Named vs unnamed: labels restructure sorting (e.g. Claude Dems +10.8 pp in T2; Gab loses appeal across the board once named).

The substantive story is the progression across the three task contexts, not the order condition. General prompts → modest party gaps; Political prompts → divergence opens up; Task 2 → divergence persists under accuracy incentives. Layered on top: brand labels sharpen the divergence, and shift its structure (e.g. naming pulls the high-recognition models in and pushes Gab out).

Result 3 — political priming carries into non-political prompts

Result 3 — political priming carries into non-political prompts

Most-preferred-model consistency in the non-political block: 61% (political-first) vs 54% (general-first) \(\beta = 0.075,\ p < .001\)

  • Among Democrats, political-first exposure decreases Gab use and increases GPT use in non-political rounds.
  • Among Republicans, the same exposure increases Grok use and decreases Claude use.
  • Identity-aligned preferences spill over into ideologically neutral content.

Once a partisan model identity is activated by political prompts, it sticks and biases subsequent choice on questions where outputs are substantively identical. Anchoring rather than reset.

Task 2 — incentivised factual choice

Single factual question: “By how many votes did Joe Biden win the 2020 popular vote?”

  • Participants choose one model before seeing the question.
  • A bonus payment is contingent on answer accuracy — strong incentive to pick the most reliable system.
  • All four pre-programmed responses are substantively identical and contain the correct answer. The unit of analysis is which model is selected, not the answer given.

This is the cleanest test we have of the tool-vs-media question: a single, factual, financially incentivised choice, with the noise from output differences engineered out of the design.

Result 4 — 71% pick their previously preferred model

Show the full figure first. Let the headline number land before pivoting to the breakdowns.

Result 4 — 71% pick their previously preferred model

Carryover Task 1 → Task 2: 71% (vs 25% under random choice; \(t = 44.6\), \(p < .001\))

  • Branding strengthens attachment: 77% carryover when models are named, 65% when not.
  • Initial GPT preferrers stick (switching 20.8%); non-GPT preferrers switch more (35.0%, \(\beta = -0.142\)).
  • GPT functions as a general fallback — switching to GPT is not differentiated by initial choice.

Even when accuracy is paid for, partisan preference dominates. The ones who switch tend to switch to the perceived-neutral model — but that pattern is uniform, not targeted at correcting specific prior choices.

Result 5 — switching is asymmetric across the ideology spectrum

Show the full figure first. Highlight the asymmetric pattern across party / ideology before unpacking the two competing interpretations.

Result 5 — switching is asymmetric across the ideology spectrum

Switching ≠ uniform. Republicans switch more than Democrats; switching rises monotonically with conservatism.

  • Republicans switch away from their preferred Task 1 model more often than Democrats.
  • Switching rises monotonically with conservatism on the continuous ideology measure.
  • Two interpretations:
    1. Right-leaning users are more responsive to accuracy incentives.
    2. They prefer right-leaning content but recognise other models as more reliable.

Persistence is widespread but not uniform. Either right-leaning users are more accuracy-responsive, or they hold a dual belief — preferring partisan content while privately acknowledging its lower factual accuracy. This asymmetry also speaks to the design caveat: with no far-left model, the rightward extreme is testable in a way the leftward extreme isn’t.

What this means for the information environment

AI systems are not interchangeable tools. Users sort across them along identity lines, and that sorting persists when accuracy is paid for.

Two possible developer equilibria

  • Tailoring: optimise for partisan audiences, deepening fragmentation.
  • Neutral positioning: compete to be the broadly trusted, accuracy-oriented default — what GPT looks like in our data.

Implication. The choice of which AI to trust may itself emerge as a new axis of polarisation. Different groups → different systems → different ways of producing and validating knowledge.

The headline isn’t just “users have biases.” It’s that AI is on track to repeat the fragmentation dynamics of cable news and social media, but in a more individualised and less socially corrigible setting.

Limitations & thanks

Limitations

  • US sample, single moment in time — patterns may differ cross-nationally and will evolve.
  • Real systems vary on dimensions our design holds fixed (interaction style, personalisation, longitudinal use).
  • Specific brand effects depend on contemporaneous public discourse — perceptions of Claude, Grok etc. may shift.
